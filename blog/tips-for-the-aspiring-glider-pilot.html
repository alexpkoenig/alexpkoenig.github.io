Tips for the aspiring glider pilot

November 2026

Having taken much longer than was warranted to earn my PPL ASEL years ago (and then after having not flown for a long time after that), as a fresh glider student I was interested in obtaining my glider rating in a club environment in a reasonably efficient manner. This guide represents tips I picked up along the way. While I write this guide purely from the perspective of a (very) rusty transition pilot, I hope it helps fresh students and transition pilots alike.

The most important thing to understand is that every day at the airfield is the most precious resource a glider pilot will have. It takes a confluence of lucky factors to make a successful gliding day happen: lengthy drives, good weather, airworthy aircraft, and available instructors, tow pilots, and field managers. For any student, the number of successful training days will be quite finite.

Moreover, by intentional design, gliding operations can be inefficient. Safety and success of operations always comes first. Some days will have everything go wrong and you can barely squeeze in a single pattern tow, only to clumsily botch the landing and feel like you’ve set yourself back a month. Other times, you will be the only student in the schedule on a day with great soaring potential. The odds of any day’s training potential are random.

Because field days are scarce and unpredictable, the best strategy is to come maximally prepared to take advantage of each day. Don’t let anything else be the bottleneck to your training.

1. Train your brain ahead of your flights.

Glider flights are short and action-packed. For an early student, you’ll get up in the air, experience a flurry of action, and then find yourself instantly back on the ground wondering what even just happened. Over time and with further flying exposure, you’ll start to get the hang of what’s going on, and be able to keep up with the glider as it flies. But it’s very possible to lay the groundwork ahead of time to make this process faster!

As a direct example, early on a student will find it quite difficult to make radio calls in the pattern while also trying to adjust glide slope, maintain proper airspeed, and scan for traffic. Over time, more of these actions will become automatic, the student will be better able to predict and anticipate their scenario, and so will not be as overloaded. Their brain’s mental model of the flight predicts what is actively unfolding, freeing their attention to tend to the flight as a whole or to strategize their next move.

Psychologically, your brain doesn’t actually perceive all of your sensory inputs in a raw, literal sense. Instead, it measures a deviation in sensory inputs relative to its predictions of the world, and the adjusted predictions are your conscious experience. As such, when your mental model of flying is thin, it is easy to become overloaded with information because of the high degree of error between your brain’s sensory inputs and its predictions.

By practicing anticipating every aspect of your flights on the ground ahead of time, you can preadapt your brain to predict the firehose of information that will come at it while in flight. What this looks like might vary from person to person, but I used a number of strategies in my training:

  • Take a first-person-POV picture of your glider’s cockpit to use at home. Memorize what the instruments look like. Practice “chair flying” by going through your checklists and other procedures with the cockpit picture displayed on a large screen. Walk through common scenarios: what does it look like when you release from tow? How about when you’re flying along and you’ve just hit a thermal? Or when you’re setting yourself up to enter the pattern and land? Or any abnormal procedures?
  • Go into Google Earth and set the perspective over your home field as if you were in the air. Drag your viewpoint around like you’re carrying out a training flight. Make note of what your view looks like and what visual markers you see on the ground. Try to estimate glide distance from various points, and calibrate yourself by actually measuring ground distance against your glider’s L/D. Think about how to adjust for the present headwinds, and any sink you might experience.
  • Practice radio procedures. Lay out a mock representation of your airport on your floor, and “fly” a pattern over it, making your radio calls as appropriate. Separately, listen to liveatc.net (which can be done at any time, even while working) to work on interpreting other people’s radio calls. Radio work will eventually become second nature, just like talking.
  • Condor is a decent resource for gaining a sight picture of gliding, and if you have a stick and rudder, for practicing the basic mechanics of flying like takeoff and tow, centering a thermal, and pattern entry and landing. Though I only used a keyboard and mouse, I would generally take it for a quick spin the night before a training day in order to freshen up on procedures.
  • Take detailed notes after each flight (much more than what would fit in a logbook description alone), review them yourself on later days, and give your recap to your next instructor to help identify areas to work on next.

2. Self-study.

Instructors and students alike will agree that verbal pedagogy at the airfield is an inefficient way to transfer the chapters of the Glider Flying Handbook and parts of the Federal Aviation Regulations (FARs) into a student’s brain. Meanwhile, instructors have a wealth of knowledge and experience that can’t be gleaned from a book alone. Self-studying ahead of time clears up the path to much more interesting ground lessons such as weather interpretation, soaring techniques, and flying safety.

The most challenging part of self-study for a well-motivated student is knowing what to study. To that end, the FAA’s Private Pilot Practical Test Standards for Glider Category is the definitive list of what you’ll be tested on during your checkride.

Resources I used early on are:

  • The Glider Flying Handbook — as a student this is your bible. It is a genuinely good read for a piece of FAA material.
  • Flight Training Manual for Gliders by Russell Holtz — contains much more detail on the practical aspects of flying than the Glider Flying Handbook alone.

Both of these can be read cover-to-cover in a week of light reading, even before your first real flying progression. Intentional and targeted self-study will pay significant dividends when you demonstrate early knowledge of the material, letting you leapfrog to more advanced concepts and skills. Later on, you should apply the same techniques to familiarizing yourself with other material, such as aeronautical charts, weather forecasts, federal aviation regulations, etcetera.

3. Understand how to read the FAR/AIM.

To earn your license you will need to be aware of much of the contents of the Federal Aviation Regulations / Aeronautical Information Manual (FAR/AIM), you will need to be knowledgeable about some of it, and you will need to have memorized some subset of that. Once you familiarize yourself with which content falls under which category, FAR/AIM study becomes a much less daunting task.

Everyone has their own studying and memorization techniques, but the best advice I got was to purchase a paper copy of the FAR/AIM, a highlighter, and sticky notes. In the FAR, read through Parts 1 (Definitions), 61 (Certification), 71 (Airspace), 91 (Flight rules), and 830 (NTSB accident reporting). Most chapters of the AIM are relevant to some degree, but pay special attention to chapters 3 (Airspace), 4 (Traffic patterns and radio phraseology), 7 (Weather and environmental factors) and 8 (Medical factors). Highlight material that is relevant to flying a glider and make comments in the margins as you go, such as writing down relevant mnemonic devices. Use sticky notes to mark routinely-referenced regulations. I am not much of a flashcard person, but I liked using a scratch book to practice deriving common rules from memory: for example, before your checkride, you will be able to draw a diagram of VFR weather minimums in various airspaces from memory, per FAR 91.155.

4. Be proactive in completing “non-flying” tasks.

There are many organizational tasks for a student to complete to eventually reach their first solo flight and then to pass a checkride. While it’s tempting to put such tasks off when future milestones don’t yet feel close, the truth of the matter is that your readiness for any particular next step will arrive without being precisely forecasted. This isn’t a conspiracy by your instructors to keep you in the dark; they can only assess when you are ready, not predict it nor plan it in advance.

Completing prerequisite paperwork at the earliest opportunity will let you open those doors more readily, such that easy organizational tasks never stand between you and your next milestone. While the exact tasks will vary per the club environment, for some examples, zero hours of flying are required in order to:

  • Apply for your student pilot certificate in IACRA.
  • Apply for a Soaring Society of America membership and obtain a glider pilot logbook.
  • Complete the SSA’s wing runner course, having memorized standard signals and operating procedures.
  • Complete airport driver training for towcart operations.

This guide won’t replace time in the air with an instructor — but it will help you as a student so that when the weather, instructor, glider, and tow plane line up, the only thing you have to worry about is your flying.
